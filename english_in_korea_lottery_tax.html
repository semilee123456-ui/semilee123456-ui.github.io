When do you owe extra tax in Korea?

If you've lived in Korea for a long stretch (typically 183 days or more per year), or your family, work, and life are mainly based here, Korea will treat you as a tax resident. This rule is the same for everyone regardless of nationality or visa type — it's not specific to any one country's citizens.

If you've only recently arrived in Korea, or you're only here temporarily, you may not owe Korean tax on this money at all. If you're unsure, ask a Korean tax accountant (세무사) directly.

Why might you owe tax in both countries?

Regardless of who wins, the US withholds 30% immediately before the money ever reaches you. Korea, meanwhile, taxes its tax residents' income no matter where in the world it was earned — so if you count as a Korean tax resident, this prize may need to be reported here too.

The US-Korea tax treaty grants relief for various other kinds of income, but there is no exemption for lottery or gambling winnings — this has been confirmed by an actual court case.

⚠️ Still unclear

Right now, nobody knows exactly how Korea will tax this money — as a simple flat add-on tax, or folded into your other income at a higher progressive rate. Even Korea's own National Tax Service acknowledges there's no settled rule for this specific scenario. If this actually happens to you, find a professional who understands both the US and Korean tax systems before you file anything.

Can the tax paid in the US reduce your Korean tax?

Yes, partially. Tax paid in the US can be credited against tax owed in Korea, so you won't be taxed in full twice on the same money. But this credit has a limit, and if the lottery prize is your only foreign-source income, exactly how that limit is calculated isn't entirely settled yet. If the amount withheld in the US exceeds the allowed limit, the excess may not be refundable.

When and how do you file?

  • In the US: after withholding, if there's a difference from the amount actually owed, it gets reconciled later via a special form (1040-NR)
  • In Korea (if you're a tax resident there): filed together with your other income in May of the year following when you received the prize

Frequently asked questions

I live in Korea. Do I have to pay tax here too?

It depends on how long you've lived in Korea and where the center of your life actually is — not your nationality. If you've spent 183 days or more a year here, you're generally treated as a tax resident and may owe tax here as well. If you're unsure, ask a tax accountant.

I'm not a US citizen or green card holder. Can I still collect the prize?

Yes, if the ticket was purchased legally. But the US will withhold 30% immediately.

If I'm a Korean tax resident, will the same money be taxed twice?

Not fully twice. The US withholds 30% first. After that, if you also owe tax in Korea, part of the tax already paid in the US reduces the Korean tax.

Can any of the tax be refunded?

The US federal withholding (30%) is almost never refunded. Some states also withhold extra tax, and depending on the state, an over-withheld portion may be refundable later.
